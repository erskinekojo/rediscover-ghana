# The present and the future: Teshie, La and Nungua (research, November 2026)

Companion to the three town dossiers. This covers what is happening now and what is being proposed. As before: built only from published sources, with confidence levels (**High / Medium / Low**), and everything needs checking on the ground. Items marked **SENSITIVE** involve live disputes, so tell them neutrally and give every party a voice.

---

## A. The fishing coast (all three towns)
- Ghana's small pelagic catch (sardinella, anchovy, mackerel: the fish the canoes mostly chase) has **fallen sharply**. Estimates differ by period and method: a USAID-backed report cites about **86%** from 1996 to the late 2010s; a Harvard Salata Institute report cites about **59%** from 1993 to 2019; a Port of Entry study about **42%** over 1998–2018 [1][2][3]. **Safe wording: "studies put the fall at somewhere between 40 and 85 percent, depending on the years measured".**
- Causes named by researchers: too many canoes, **light fishing**, industrial trawlers catching fish they aren't licensed for, and climate variability [1][3].
- The Harvard report says traditional canoe fishers earned an average of about **US$7 per trip by 2021** (Ghana-wide, not these towns) [2]. Medium.
- Scientists warned that stocks could collapse without a big cut in fishing pressure. In 2025 the Fisheries Minister exempted canoe fishers from that year's closed season; an advocate said canoes catch about 70% of small pelagics, so the exemption weakens the closed season [3][4]. **SENSITIVE: fishers' livelihoods vs. conservation. Interview both fishers and fisheries officers.**
- **Fuel:** at Nungua in June 2025 the Krowor municipal chief executive said premix fuel supply was still short, and outboard motors were not yet available [5]. Medium.
- None of these sources are specific to Teshie, La or Nungua. **The towns' own fishers are the real source. Ask them what the catch was when they started.**

## B. The sea is coming in
- A University of Ghana study of the **Teshie shoreline, 1986–2016,** measured change from about **−2.5 m a year (retreat) to +2.9 m a year (gain)** in different stretches. It names sea-level rise, tides, the shape of the coast, **sand mining** and population growth as causes, and links erosion to **lost landing sites and lower catches** [6]. Medium.
- A climate envoy said in April 2026 that **Nungua, Teshie, La and Osu** are losing homes, buildings and some schools to high tides [7]. Medium (one report).
- **Labadi:** a traditional priest, **Togbe Akpasso,** told MyJoyOnline (2 April 2026) that the sea is eating into the land around a shrine he built more than 30 years ago. He said, "Without government help, we cannot do anything" [7]. Medium. Great material: ask him on camera.
- Ada, east of Tema, has had a sea-defence project (breakwaters); I found nothing on its results [6]. A possible comparison for the "future" chapter.

## C. La: the beach (SENSITIVE, live and fast-moving)
Facts as reported in October 2026. **Check for updates the week you record.**
- **2 October 2026, about 4 a.m.:** a joint military and police team (reported as the **48 Engineer Regiment** with the Ghana Police) began demolishing structures at **La Pleasure Beach**; the **Polo Beach Club** was also demolished [8][9]. Graphic's opinion writer says it was supervised by National Security at the request of **Labadi Beach Hotel** [10]. Reports differ on who supervised: **say "security forces"**.
- **Who owns the land is disputed.** The MP, **Rita Naa Odoley Sowah,** says she was told it belongs to Labadi Beach Hotel; the **La Traditional Council** calls itself the traditional landowner; a community lawyer says it belongs to the people of La [8][11][12].
- **The Council's account (7 Oct):** it was **not formally notified**, and SSNIT and National Security apologised for not engaging it. It wants a halt until consultation, and compensation and space for vendors after any redevelopment [11].
- **The MP alleges** some personnel fired shots; the MP says she was told the demolition was planned only after assurances it would not happen [9]. **These are allegations. Attribute them.**
- **SSNIT's stated plan (12 Oct):** SSNIT (which owns Labadi Beach Hotel and La Palm Royal Beach Hotel) said it intends an organised leisure and tourism destination (restaurants, beach lounges, water sports, wellness, retail), that the beach "will remain accessible as a public beach," that La residents have "a legitimate interest," and that work starts "after engagement with stakeholders" [13].
- **Traders:** the Ghana Tourism Federation (GHATOF) says **85 businesses** were demolished and about **4,000 jobs, largely held by women,** are at risk, many businesses having operated for decades with permits. It wants compensation and "first right of refusal" on future space [14]. **These are the Federation's figures.**
- **Law:** a community lawyer (19 Oct) said legal action had started over "unauthorised occupation"; no court or case number was given [12].
- **Status:** headlines suggest the redevelopment plans were **suspended pending consultation** and that victims would receive compensation. **I could not read those articles; treat as unverified** [14].
- **Politics:** several politicians have commented. **Leave party politics out of the film.**
- **Context (not the beach):** an SSNIT-funded national convention centre was announced for the Trade Fair site, not the beach. Don't link them in narration [10].

## D. Nungua: jobs and a wharf
- **Factory (14 Oct 2026):** the Nungua Traditional Stool Authority has partnered with **WT Plastics** to build a plastics factory on stool land at **Borteyman,** near the Accra–Tema Motorway. Stated: **400 direct jobs** (priority for Nungua indigenes) and **over 1,000 indirect** (the "1,400" headline is the sum). Chief of Nungua Sangonaa, Nii Sango, called it a step against youth unemployment. No investment amount, date or output given [15]. **Single source. Ask the stool.**
- **Wharf:** the Krowor Municipal Assembly has put a **wharf at Nungua Landing Beach** into its Medium Term Development Plan. The MCE said "most of the big canoes prefer to go to Tema." No cost or date [5]. **A plan, not a build.**
- **Size:** the assembly's budget estimates Krowor's population at about **143,000** (2021 census basis) [16]. (Wikipedia's 84,119 is a 2012 figure for Nungua alone.) Use the assembly figure with attribution.
- **Nshɔbulemɔ 2025:** fishers performed the rite to pacify the sea before Kplejoo, hoping to land **tsile** for the kpokpoi [5].

## E. Teshie: the present
- Teshie's schools and institutions include the **Ghana Military Academy** and **KAIPTC** (peacekeeping training centre) [17]. Fort Augustaborg is in ruins [17].
- **Homowo 2025:** clashes at the kpokpoi-sprinkling day left **two to three dead** (reports differ). The Teshie Traditional Council condemned the violence and said the festival had been "desecrated." The Mantse, **Gbetsoolo Nii Ashitey Akomfra III,** announced a "safer, bigger" 2026 with police and Asafo groups cooperating [18]. **SENSITIVE. Only include with the Council's guidance.**
- The **Kane Kwei Carpentry Workshop** is run by Seth Kane Kwei's grandson, **Eric Adjetey Anang.** Kane Kwei coffins have been shown at museums in Europe, and Anang had a solo show in Arkansas in 2020 [19]. The workshop's own site says "almost 10 workshops" make such coffins today (self-reported, undated) [20].

## F. Possibilities (what is on the table, not predictions)
- **Heritage tourism:** the **Ghana Tourism Authority** pledged in May 2025 to help make **Homowo a marquee cultural-tourism event.** The Ga Mantse said beaches, forts and castles could become revenue drivers "with proper refurbishment and promotion" and linked Homowo to ending hunger. **No budget or results published** [21]. A 2026 preprint notes that few studies measure whether festival tourism actually benefits communities [22].
- **Craft to the world:** fantasy coffins already travel to museums; the workshop model could carry apprentices [19][20].
- **Fisheries:** options in the literature are fewer canoes, closed seasons, better landing sites (the Nungua wharf idea) and diversifying income [1][2]. **Ask the fishers what they would want.**
- **Coast:** sea defences exist elsewhere (Ada). Whether and where they come is unknown [6].
- **Memory:** oral histories are held by elders. Recording them (what this project is for) is itself a possibility.
